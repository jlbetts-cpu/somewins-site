How it’s designed

Designed for ADHD minds, one decision at a time.

Every part of Some Wins is a UX decision made for how ADHD shapes a day. Here is each one, why it is there, and the research behind it.

  • A win in one tap.

    A win goes in from the camera on Wins, a widget, or the Some Wins camera on your Lock Screen, and lands on your tower the moment you log it.

    With ADHD, waiting weighs more, and in adults it is part of why things get put off.

    Netzer Turgeman & Pollak, 2026, Journal of Attention Disorders

  • Nothing to lose.

    No streak to keep alive and no red. Plan lines you didn’t get to wait under Earlier, never marked missed, until you move them to today or let them go.

    When a streak breaks, people tend to do less afterwards, not more.

    Silverman & Barasch, 2023, Journal of Consumer Research

  • A small day is a whole day.

    The daily goal starts at two. The ring only fills, and Wins shows just the next row of empty slots, so a day of two wins looks finished.

    Steadying hard feelings is often harder for adults with ADHD, so the app never shows a shortfall.

    Soler-Gutiérrez et al., 2023, PLOS ONE

  • Three for today, picked fresh.

    Up to three lines are the day’s three, above the rest of the plan. They belong to their day, so each morning starts with three new ones to pick.

    Few enough to see at once, small enough to finish.

    Our own call. We found no study of the right number.

  • Plans that say when.

    Write a time into a line, like Call Grandma @6pm. It goes on your tower and your Lock Screen as it nears, and a reminder comes right then.

    Doing things at the right time is harder with ADHD, and if-then plans, when this happens I’ll do that, are among the best-tested ways to act on what you meant to do.

    Seesjärvi et al., 2025, Scientific Reports · Sheeran et al., 2024, European Review of Social Psychology

  • A first step for a big line.

    First Step puts a smaller line above a big one, asking for the smallest way in. Ticking it is a win of its own.

    Putting things off goes with ADHD across studies. Starting is the hard part, so starting is what counts.

    Suriano, 2026, Research in Developmental Disabilities

  • Time you can see.

    Focus shows the time left as a ring, on its button, on your Lock Screen and in the Dynamic Island, then rings at the end and asks for the win.

    People with ADHD judge time less accurately, across ages.

    Marx et al., 2022, JAACAP · Zheng et al., 2022, Journal of Attention Disorders

  • The journal starts from your photos.

    By evening your photos are already on your tower, so the High, the Low and the Buffalo are a tap each. Words are optional.

    Noting what went well is a gratitude practice, and those give a small lift in well-being.

    Choi et al., 2025, PNAS

  • Close friends, no feed.

    A crew is the few people you choose. No feed, no likes to chase, and tonight’s story is gone by morning.

    Young people with ADHD report more loneliness than their peers, and many neurodivergent people start or stay on a task with someone else there.

    Jong et al., 2024, Journal of Attention Disorders · Eagle et al., 2024, ACM TACCESS

  • One nudge a day, at most.

    One reminder to log, timed to when you usually post, never after 10pm, none once you’ve logged a win, and quiet by itself after three days without an answer.

    A phone’s notification sound slowed people on the task in front of them.

    Upshaw et al., 2022, PLOS ONE
